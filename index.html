The Tale of Reynard

The straight line.

Centuries before the tale of Reynard, an ancient mathematician named Archimedes formally wrote down an axiom: of all lines which have the same extremities, the straight line is the least. Fast-forward to medieval times: a lion king summons a fox named Reynard. But instead of pleading, he decided to upset the court. Today, that ember still burns brightly.

The timeline

225 BCE — The axiom

Archimedes formalizes what every road-builder already knew: of all lines which have the same extremities, the straight line is the least. Common sense, written down.

c. 1175 — The tale

Across Europe, ordinary people tell stories of Reynard the Fox — small against kings and courts, who never petitions the powerful and simply does what should be done: he routes around them. The tales are satire, and that is the politics — among the first stories common people used to laugh at power. They loved him because he was obviously right.

Today — The token

Glenride is a think tank in the traditional structure — manuscripts, peer review, rigor, scholars named for their work. But ideologically separate: no tower decides what gets read or who gets honored. Independence needs a mechanism, because recognition can't run on goodwill. SPIN is that mechanism — and it is a promise: that those who truly move the world with research will be honored for it. Not by the court. By the commons. The straight line, drawn a third time.

❦

Who is Reynard?

In the medieval tales, Reynard was the fox who outwitted kings — summoned to the lion’s court, condemned by wolves, hunted by bears, and he talked his way out every single time. Not with force. With wit. The powerful built their courts and wrote their rules. Reynard built his exits. Eight hundred years later, the court is publishers and the walls are paywalls. The fox is back.
